One of my long-standing travel goals had been to to visit some of the classic old american cities in the South. 

In 2016, I had the opportuntity to visit two of these: Savannah and also, on a short side-trip, Charleston.

This was my first trip to the American South, and I was really interested to learn more about the culture and history of its old cities first hand.

Prior to the Civil War, the US South had not seen anything like the urbanisation seen in parts of the North. Throughout the South, only a few cities had emerged: Savannah, Charleston, Nashville, New Orleans, Memphis, etc. Much of the South was agricultural, with much land owned by large wealthy landowners and worked by enslaved populations. There was also a significant amount of small land-holding. Cities like Savannah and Charleston became famous for their osentatious mansions, some of which continue to be maintained as interesting house museums.

Savannah is also a rare example of a planned American city. It was founded with a network of squares surrounded by churches, mansions and other impressive buildings - a scheme that has been preserved to the present.

Interestingly, while Savannah became one of the classic cities for wealthy owners of enslaved people, slavery was actually not originally permitted in Georgia when the state was first founded.

Today, it is fascinating to walk around the historic area of the City. Impressive antebellum mansions stand beside many other interesting buildings with architectural details such as outstanding cast-iron construction. Former quarters for enslaved people are still extant, featuring interesting details such as the use of "haint blue", a sort of light blue derived from indigo that was used on porch ceilings and originated among the Gullah peoples. The use colour has its origins in folk beliefs regarding warding off evil spirits.

The form of slavery that developed in the South was an extremely brutal version even compared to other societies that also enslaved people. Distinctive elements of the american way of slavery included its absolute racial caste system, the harsh treatment of the enslaved, practices such as family seperation, where members of families could be sold to different people, laws against schooling for enslaved people, and the rule that people born to an enslaved mother inherited their mother's enslaved status. Many of these harsh practices were actually becoming worse over time in the pre-civil war years.

This latter point was consistent with roman practices, where there was a legal doctrine of "partus sequitur ventrem". In the US South, this meant that people enlsaved their own children - as children of an enslaved woman and her owner became enslaved. A famous example of this was Jefferson, who had a number of enslaved children who were eventually granted free status after years of slavery. This rule obviously led to many situations where people who may not have looked african-american were nevertheless marked as enslaved based on their maternal lineage. As an aside, many americans are not aware of this feature of slavery. I was on a house tour when one person asked about this, and no one, including the docent, appeared aware of the south's "partus sequitur ventrem" rule.

However, manumission was possible - and oddly, populations of free black people co-existed with larger populations of enslaved people in the South. This was especially true in New Orleans.

In terms of family seperation, there was an infamous event in Savannah, known as "the weeping time", when over 400 enslaved people were auctioned to raise money to cover gambling debts of an owner of enslaved pepole. 

Savannah also has a glorious major park close to its downtown: Forsyth Park, which features a distinctive cast-iron fountain. It also has a dominating statue of a confederate soldier. Throughout the southern cities I visited I saw many such civil war memorials - often refering to "the war between the states" or even "the war of northern aggression".

One thing I had't known about was the Irish history in Savannah. Apparently, a significant number of Irish people from the North would regularly travel there for seasonal work.
