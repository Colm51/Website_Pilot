Mérida is the colouful capital of the Yucutan, and my base for exploring this area, along with the neighbouring states of Campeche and Quintana Roo.

This part of Mexico had a very different colonial-era and pre-civil war era history than much of the rest of the nation. It was distant from Mexico City, and largely independent from the federal government. It actually had closer ties to the US south and even Europe than to other parts of the country.

Its economy and social order was also distinctive. Land ownership was extremely concentrated in the hands of a small number of families. Social conditions fostered a great deal of discontent, and the region has seen a series of extremely violent indigenous revolts during different periods. The main example of this was the Caste War which extended throughout the second half of the 19th Century into the first part of the 20th century. This was a complex prolonged conflict, involving various groups of Maya, rivalry between elites in the cities of Mérida and Campeche, the involvement of federal forces, as well as economic changes occuring with the large-scale adoption of Henequen cultivation.

Henequen was a crop whose fibres were a major source of raw material for making rope. With the increasing demand for rope for many industrial uses, this crop became extremely valuable, leading to immense fortunes for a small number of large landowners. Evidence of the weath of these families is still evident in the grandiose mansions and palazzos that still line the Paseo de Montejo.

Mérida was built on the site of a Mayan community, and there are still remnnants of that earlier settlement apparent in the stones used in the construction of colonial churches.

Today, the city is an extremely colourful and evocastive place to explore. Its many pastel-coloured houses and other structures make for intriguing walks.

It was also known for having a colourful Mardis Gras. Sadly, this event was relocated by authorities to a location in the suburbs, supposedly at the behest of merchants along the traditional parade route along the Paseo de Montejo. When I was there, there was little evidence of any mardis gras activity despite it being the time of year when these festivities occured.
