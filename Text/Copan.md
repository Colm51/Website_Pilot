In 2022, I travelled for the first time since covid. 

I decided to explore Honduras, including spending a week learning scuba on Utila and another week in Copàn Ruinas, a picturesque colonial-era town that abuts a famous Mayan site.

It turned out that travelling to Copàn Ruinas was challenging owing both to road blockades and also extreme rainy weather that had washed out roads.

During the days, I studied Spanish at a local school before lunch and then explored the town and the neighbouring mayan site.

The site of Copàn was once thought to have been a centre of peaceable astronomers. Subsequent archaeology confirmed it was deeply enmeshed in the inter-city rivalries and warfare of the classic mayan era.

There continue to be debates about what led to the decline of Copàn and other lowland Mayan cities around 800-900 AD. One line of analysis that seems plausible emphasizes various environmental catastrophes that made these sites uninhabitable. Environmental research has documented that there were prolonged periods of droughts in some areas. In other areas such as Copàn, severe deforestation may have led to catastrophic flooding and loss of arable land.

The building of the enormous Mayan temples and other structures took an enormous toll on the environment. Vast amounts of timber were used in the production of the materials used in the construction of these structures. Today, these areas are often in the midst of dense jungles. But but by the end of the classic mayan era there had been extensive deforestation.

That said, theories of the mayan world have often proven to be incomplete or simply inaacurate. And knowledge of the period has continued to make impressive progress.

As Michael Coe writes in Breaking the Maya Code, for many years, the orthodox archaological opinion was that the Mayan were peaceable, focused on things like astronomy. And the glyphs found at the sites were seen as inscrutable, with images somehow representing complete thoughts. As progress was made deciphering the glyphs, this was found to be totally incorrect. Many of the glyphs represented sounds, and familiarity with related languages and spanish-era texts eventually led to an impressive reconstruction of the written language. It was found to refer often to kings and warfare.

More recently, LIDAR has uncovered many previously-unexplored ruins, and it seems likely that the theories that have evolved about the relations among the mayan city-states will continue to need to be significantly revised.

Copàn is an evocative site, with well-preserved stellae, stone carvings and a ball court.

I also visited a coffee farm near to Copàn, and despite the wet weather, had a very interesting tour of an active coffee operation.
